## Decisions
- 2026-09-12: gave the page budget two lanes — the walk and what the sitemaps declare — spent one request for one, superseding the single queue that enqueued every sitemap URL at depth 0 ahead of every link. Measured with `npm run analyze` against kjell.com at 80 pages: before, all 80 pages fetched were sitemap product URLs and not one carried a query string; after, 40 were walked — the seed, category and outlet listings, `?outlet=a` filter variants, the `/no` locale — and `canonicalization` went from 1 failure to 40, because a page with no rel=canonical here is exactly the kind of page no sitemap lists. 2.8 moved from passed to held for the same reason: it was clear only because the page that warns had never been fetched
- 2026-09-12: let a lane that runs dry hand what is left of the budget to the other, rather than reserving half for each, so a site with no sitemap is walked exactly as it was and a sitemap smaller than its share is still read whole — the split only binds when both lanes have more than the budget can take, which is the only case that was ever broken
- 2026-09-12: kept the walk first in the rotation, so the seed is still the first page fetched: the root document is what the icon requests, the TLS handshake and the sink’s first row are taken from, and none of that should move because of how the rest of the budget is divided
- 2026-09-12: restated the sink’s ordering guarantee as "a page arrives after the page it was linked from" rather than "breadth-first from seeds", because interleaving the lanes breaks the second and not the first — a page’s links are enqueued only once it has been fetched and awaited, which is the property `discoveredFromId` actually resolves against
- 2026-09-11: moved 3.9 from `automated` to `assisted`, signed off by the maintainer, under the same 2026-09-04 rule that moved 3.11: its "Done when" asks that content *answers* its main query, that claims *needing* support carry sources, and that bylines and dates appear where readers would *reasonably expect* them — three editorial judgements, where markup shows only that headings, bylines and dates exist. Edited in v4.4 rather than cut as a new version because neither detector existed, so no audit had ever graded 3.9. It is not a launch gate, so less rode on it than on 3.11, but a machine pass is a clearance either way. Automated checks: 42 to 41
- 2026-09-11: split 3.9 by how many pages each question needs: `answer-first-structure` reads one page's reading matter, `author-date-signals` reads the site's articles together, because the failure the corpus names outright — bylines and dates "as site-wide boilerplate" — is a fact about many pages that no page can see about itself, which is why 2.7's detector is site-scoped too
- 2026-09-11: identified an article only by what the page declares — a schema.org Article-family type in structured data, or `og:type=article` with an `article:published_time` — and never by `og:type` alone, because WordPress SEO plugins set that on every singular page, the contact page included, and judging bylines there would bury the finding under pages nobody expects one on. The first draft took structured data only, and `npm run analyze` against smashingmagazine.com and wordpress.org/news found both publish without any JSON-LD and declare every article as an Open Graph article with a publication time; they read `not-applicable`. A site whose articles declare neither still does, and whether they ought to is a person's question
- 2026-09-11: asked for an author only on pages typed as articles in structured data, while judging the dates of Open Graph articles too, because an Open Graph article with a publication time is also what a static-site template writes on every dated page: the second live run found Smashing Magazine's ebook-bundle product pages declared that way, and seven "names no author" warnings on shop pages were the byline-forcing the corpus tells sites not to do
- 2026-09-11: failed only dates that are false on their face — a value that is not a date (an unrendered `{{ post.updated }}`), a modification more than a day before publication, a date more than a day after the server's own `Date` header — and made a missing byline, a missing date and a stamp a `warn`, because whether readers expect a byline on a given article is the judgement 3.9 leaves to a person. The day of slack keeps a timezone from reading as a contradiction; the `Date` header rather than the analysing machine's clock lets a replayed crawl reach the verdict it reached the day it ran
- 2026-09-11: called a date a template's stamp only when one value carrying a time of day is shared by at least five articles and four in five of those declaring one, because a news site publishes many stories on one date, while one value identical to the second across most of an archive is a build, not an editor
- 2026-09-11: had `answer-first-structure` never fail: 600 words under no subheading and a question heading with nothing beneath it are `warn`s, because the second is also what an FAQ whose answers arrive by script looks like in a raw crawl (Phase 5), and whether the text answers anything is the question the check leaves to a person. Only headings in the reading matter count — the main landmark, a lone article, or the body without navigation, asides and page chrome — since a menu's headings signpost nothing on the page
- 2026-09-11: counted a heading as a question only by its question mark, because "How to fit a tap" is a task answered by whatever steps sit beneath it, and reading interrogative words as questions would demand an answer paragraph from headings that already have one in the form of their subsections
- 2026-09-11: moved 3.11 from `automated` to `assisted`, by the triage's own 2026-09-04 rule that a row is automated only when its "Done when" closes on observation alone — 3.11's asks that media alternatives are *accurate* and content understandable, and markup shows only that they exist. With one detector behind the check, keeping it automated would have let a markup pass clear a launch gate nobody had read, which is the 2026-09-07 `ai-crawler-directive-verify` problem again. Edited in v4.4 rather than cut as a new version because no audit had ever graded 3.11 — its detector did not exist — so no delivered verdict moves with it. Automated checks: 43 to 42
- 2026-09-11: had `content-accessibility` report every barrier 3.11 names that markup can show, including missing alt, missing `lang` and uncaptioned video, which `image-alt-quality`, `lang-attribute` and `media-alternatives` also read — those serve 2.2, 1.14 and 1.9, and 3.11 is graded by this detector alone, so leaving them out would let the check be proposed as clear on a page a screen reader cannot use. One fact, two checks, two reasons
- 2026-09-11: failed only what WCAG level A fails outright (a link with no accessible name, an image with no alt, no page language) and made the rest a `warn` for a person: generic link text is allowed when its sentence gives the purpose (2.4.4), a table without headers may be layout, and a `<video>` without a caption track may have its captions burned into the picture. Checked against live pages: w3.org/WAI, gov.uk, bbc.com/news and kjell.com pass; arngren.net fails on 188 image links whose only image has `alt=""`
- 2026-09-11: computed a link's accessible name in the extractor, in the order the accessible-name algorithm tries (`aria-label`, `aria-labelledby`, text plus contained image alt, `title`), rather than reading `anchorText`, because an icon link with an `aria-label` is perfectly named and has no text at all — judging by text would fail every well-built social-links footer
- 2026-09-11: learned a host's HTTP version from a TLS handshake offering `h2` and `http/1.1` rather than from any fetch, because Node's `fetch` speaks HTTP/1.1 whatever the server offers — every page the crawl ever fetched was HTTP/1.1 by our choice, not the site's. The handshake is what a browser does to decide, sends no request, and is made by the crawl loop under the same delay and signal as every other visit
- 2026-09-11: read HTTP/3 from an `Alt-Svc` header on the root document rather than attempting QUIC, because that header is how a browser learns to use HTTP/3 in the first place, and Node has no stable QUIC client; an advertised `h3` passes even where the handshake chose HTTP/1.1, since the browser switches on the header
- 2026-09-11: handshook with the host the root document landed on after redirects, not the seed spelling, because the version a visitor gets is the canonical host's; and accepted any certificate for it, because a certificate problem has already failed every page fetch and the question here is only which protocol the server picks
- 2026-09-11: made HTTP/1.1 alone a `warn` rather than a `fail`, alongside missing compression and missing security headers in the same check, which are held the same way; and reported a crawl with no handshake recorded as `error`, because absent is not HTTP/1.1 — a replayed crawl, or one with auxiliary requests off, simply did not ask
- 2026-09-11: committed a self-signed localhost certificate in @seo/testkit for the handshake tests rather than generating one at test time, because Node cannot mint an X.509 certificate without openssl, and CI and a Windows shell do not agree on where that is; it is valid for `localhost` and 127.0.0.1 only and guards nothing
- 2026-09-11: split 1.12 into a crawl question and an index question — `parameter-crawl-space` asks whether the parameter URL space is bounded, `faceted-nav-control` whether each filtered page has a decision behind it — because they fail independently: every filter noindexed and ten thousand of them linked, or twelve filters linked and every one an indexable duplicate of its listing
- 2026-09-11: had `faceted-nav-control` judge that each filtered page carries a decision, not which decision, because the corpus leaves the choice of landing pages to the site; what a machine can see is a filter that is indexable with no canonical (nobody decided), one kept out of the index and still submitted in the sitemap (two decisions that disagree), and one that declares itself a landing page while carrying its listing's title (a decision that is not true)
- 2026-09-11: let neither 1.12 detector pass on filter URLs the crawl found and did not fetch, holding the check with `warn` instead — whether a parameter space closes is only observed by walking it, and a filter URL left in the frontier might lead nowhere or to a thousand more. `pagination-crawl-path` set the precedent for "linked but not reached". Found against kjell.com, where the first draft passed 23 unfetched filter URLs as bounded
- 2026-09-11: recorded URLs linked past `maxDepth` in `CrawlResult.notReached` rather than dropping them, because the depth budget is a limit of ours like the page budget and the field already promised "discovered but not fetched because a budget ran out"; stacked filters live exactly one click past wherever the crawl stops, so a crawl reporting nothing left over there was describing its own horizon as the site's edge. Kept out of `queued`, so the walk itself is unchanged
- 2026-09-11: recognised a session identifier only by names with no other meaning (`jsessionid`, `phpsessid`, `sessionid` and a few platform-specific ones), not `sid` or `session`, because those are a section or a conference session on enough sites that failing them would teach people to ignore the finding
- 2026-09-11: failed a crawl budget spent on parameter variants only when they are at least ten URLs and half of what was left unfetched, and only variants of routes the crawl already fetched; ten is a floor against a crawl that happened to end beside a sort link, and the route test keeps a crawl that ran out of *documents* from reading as one that ran out on permutations
- 2026-09-11: read campaign parameters from the raw href on internal links rather than from crawled URLs, because normalization strips them — the crawl never saw them as separate addresses, and every other crawler does; they are a `warn`, since they are as many as there are links rather than unbounded
- 2026-09-11: read "resource contention" as contention for the thing an audit borrows, not the machine it runs on — the site being crawled. The machine's side was already bounded by `concurrency`; what was not bounded was two leased workers, each with a free slot and a free lane in its own memory, crawling one site together. That became possible when leases let a second worker share a namespace (2026-09-08) and nothing had caught up with it
- 2026-09-11: laned audits on the host rather than the origin, superseding the 2026-09-04 origin lane: every crawl requests all four scheme/www spellings of its seed and follows redirects between them, so `http://example.com` and `https://www.example.com` were two lanes reaching one server. A non-default port stays in the key because a service on its own port is usually its own process, and other subdomains stay apart because the crawl never leaves its host and `blog.` is often another platform
- 2026-09-11: put the cross-worker lane check in the store (`JobStore.acquire`) rather than in a Postgres lock the queue holds for the length of a run, because a session-level advisory lock pins a pooled connection for minutes per audit and dies with the connection rather than with the lease; the jobs table already says who is running what and whether their claim is live, so the lane is a question about rows that already exist and needs no migration
- 2026-09-11: serialized `acquire` with a transaction-scoped advisory lock on `(queue, lane)`, because a check-then-write under READ COMMITTED lets two workers asking at once each read no running job and each write their own — the race test fails three runs in three without it
- 2026-09-11: let only a *live* claim hold a lane, so a worker that dies mid-crawl stops holding its site when its lease ages out, exactly when its job becomes anybody's; waiting on the row instead would make one crash stall a site until someone cleaned the table
- 2026-09-11: excluded the asking worker's own jobs from the lane check, since its in-memory lanes already serialize them and a finished job's row outlives its run by one best-effort write — counting it would stall the next audit of that site behind a delete
- 2026-09-11: made a lane refusal cost no attempt and wait one heartbeat, and made a store that cannot answer count as a refusal: running on the store's silence is the second crawler this exists to prevent, and a store unreachable for long is one whose leases are expiring anyway
- 2026-09-11: had `load` write recovered jobs back as `queued`, because a row left reading `running` by a dead process would hold its lane against every other worker for as long as the job waited for a slot — possibly the whole of a retry backoff
- 2026-09-11: kept lanes per queue namespace rather than global, because lease freshness is a property of the namespace's own `leaseMs` and one namespace per queue is already the rule
- 2026-09-11: recognised a gzip sitemap by its magic bytes rather than by `Content-Type` or a `.gz` suffix, because servers label the file `application/gzip`, `x-gzip`, `octet-stream` and occasionally `text/xml`, and a URL suffix says what someone named the file, not what the server sent; the two bytes are the only thing every gzip file agrees on
- 2026-09-11: applied the sitemap ceiling to the expanded size, as the protocol does, which also bounds a small file that inflates without end — measuring the compressed size would have let 50 MB of gzip expand to gigabytes in memory
- 2026-09-11: read a damaged gzip file as far as it decompresses and marked it truncated, rather than failing the fetch, because the server did answer and `error` means no response was obtained; detectors already report a truncated sitemap as unobservable. A connection that drops mid-file is still a failed fetch — the two are told apart by which side of the decompressor the error came from
- 2026-09-11: made `byteLength` of a truncated body a lower bound rather than the full size, since knowing the full size meant downloading it, and not downloading it is what the limit is for; nothing downstream judges a page by its size, and `truncated` says the number is partial
- 2026-09-11: stopped binary bodies at the same limit as textual ones, because the walk follows links and a link to a video file was being read whole into memory only to be measured
- 2026-09-11: raised the body limit for sitemaps alone, to sitemaps.org's 50 MB, which the 2026-09-08 decision declined to do for bodies in general: a page limit is our guess and raising it only moves the cliff, but this ceiling is the protocol's, so a sitemap read up to it has been read as far as any consumer is promised. Found by `npm run analyze` against ign.com, whose 4–7 MB quarterly video sitemaps left 2.14 and 2.1 at `probe-error`
- 2026-09-11: parsed sitemaps as a stream of SAX events rather than buffering them for cheerio, because a DOM of a 50 MB file costs several times that in memory for a result that is only a list; what the parser holds is now the entry it is inside
- 2026-09-11: dropped the entry a cut severed instead of returning it, since a severed `<loc>` is half a URL that went into the crawl frontier and a severed video entry is a field "missing" by our cut; the document is still marked truncated, because what was read is still partial
- 2026-09-08: replaced the sleeps that decided a retry had not fired with assertions on the queue’s own state — the job is out of the queue and the queue is idle, so no later moment can run it — because a bare sleep raced the queue’s own timers (a 50ms backoff against vitest’s 50ms waitFor poll) and failed once in five runs. Waiting on the retrying event rather than on state === queued also removed a second ambiguity: a job reads as queued both before its first attempt and while it waits out a backoff
- 2026-09-08: counted a `<video>` element as a video whether or not the markup says where its media comes from, after `npm run analyze` against euronews.com found eight pages with `<video class="js-native-video">` and no `src` — the source is assigned in script. Deriving "there is a video here" from a URL rather than from the element made all three detectors silent on exactly the pages that most needed them, and the site's 2.14 came back a false pass
- 2026-09-08: kept the markup-vs-player comparison on the URLs the markup does state, since a player sourced in script names no video to disagree about; it is the *count* that a sourceless player contributes, which is what "plays more videos than it describes" needs
- 2026-09-09: gave each sitemap declared in robots.txt a lane of its own and spent the fifty-document budget round-robin across them, after `npm run analyze` against ign.com read 510,000 article URLs and none of the 440 video entries sitting in a single quarterly file — `sitemap-videos.xml` is declared fourth and the article index ahead of it has more children than the budget. What a site declares first is not what an audit needs most
- 2026-09-09: accepted that the change reads fewer URLs from the largest sitemap (IGN: 510k to 181k), because a sample of every declared sitemap answers more checks than an exhaustive read of one; the budget exists to bound requests, and nothing in the corpus asks for a complete URL inventory
